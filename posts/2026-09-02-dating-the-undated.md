# Dating the Undated: Newspaper Radio Logs as Primary Evidence for a Quarter-Million Broadcast Recordings

**Date**: 2026-09-02  
**Author**: radio index  
**Tags**: Research Paper, Discography, Broadcast Dating, Newspaper Radio Logs, Sound Records, Audio Archives, ARSC

---

## Abstract

Many old American radio recordings survive today. Collectors trade the recordings as sound
files. A large number of the recordings carry no date. Often the only information about a
recording is the file name. File names do not last. People write the file name again at
every copy. Computers cut the file name short. When a file name is hard to read, a
cataloger chooses a date. So the date is lost, and many surviving dates are wrong.

The paper describes a method for finding the true date of a radio recording. The main
evidence is the newspaper radio log — the daily list of radio programs printed in American
newspapers. The method compares radio logs from several separate newspapers. The method
also uses trade magazines, and evidence inside the recording.

The paper reports what the method produced at The Archive of American Radio. The Archive of
American Radio holds 259,915 broadcast records across 6,114 programs. Twenty-eight percent
of the 259,915 records still have no date.

The paper also proposes a way to mark how strong the evidence is for each date. The mark
separates a date proved by several independent listings from a date a cataloger only chose,
and from a date passed along by another collection and never tested.

The paper gives an example. Six independent listings move the date of a *Greatest Story
Ever Told* recording by almost seven years. The day of the week cannot settle the question,
because both possible dates fall on a Sunday, and *Greatest Story Ever Told* aired on
Sundays.

The paper reports a limit as well. In the sample studied, the number of listings available
for a broadcast rises sharply with the year across the 1930s. The measure of the rise is a
correlation of 0.899 across fourteen records. Evidence is not equally thick across the whole
period. Any dating system must show where evidence is thick and where evidence is thin, and must not hide the difference behind
a flat claim of fact.

**Keywords:** old-time radio, discography, broadcast dating, newspaper radio logs, sound
records, audio archives, metadata

## 1. The problem: a body of evidence that lost its dates

Old American radio survives mainly because collectors saved old American radio. People
recorded the programs on transcription discs and later on tape. People copied the copies.
People traded the copies, and in time made the copies into computer files. The trading went
on for about seventy years. Across seventy years, no library and no archive set a rule for
the information that traveled with each recording. The result is a large body of valuable
history with almost no reliable record keeping.

The clearest failure is the date. A recording without a date is much weaker as evidence. A
reader cannot place an undated recording against the news of the week. A reader cannot
connect an undated recording to an advertiser's campaign. A reader cannot say where an
undated recording falls inside a program's run. A reader cannot use an undated recording to
show what a network really broadcast on a given night. Radio was live and scheduled, and
then radio was gone. So the date is not a detail among many. The date is what turns a sound
file into a historical document.

Dates disappear in several different ways. Separating the ways helps, because each way needs
a different answer.

**File names break down.** In trading networks, the file name carries the information.
People write the file name again at every transfer to fit a new collector's system.
Computers cut long file names short. Some letters are not legal on some computers, so people
remove the letters. Computer programs for cataloging change other letters. A date written as
`xx-xx-xx` may survive a single copy. A date written as `xx-xx-xx` does not survive three
copies.

**Wrong dates are passed along.** When a date is present, the date is often passed along
rather than proved. A collector chooses a date that looks reasonable. Every later collector copies the chosen date forward. Over time the chosen date starts to look official, only
because so many collectors repeat the chosen date. Nothing in a trading network ever removes
a wrong date.

**Programs are dated, but recordings are not.** Many holdings carry only the years a program
ran, such as 1952 to 1961. A run of years is a fact about the program. A run of years is not
a fact about the recording.

**Chosen dates are stored as facts.** When a cataloger is not sure, the chosen date goes
into the same field, in the same form, as a proved date. Nothing marks the difference
between a chosen date and a proved date. So the difference disappears at the first copy.

The paper treats the last failure as the main problem, because a careful method can repair
the last failure. The Association for Recorded Sound Collections states the principle
clearly in *Guidelines for Discographies*: "estimates are preferable to omitting the
information. Discographers should explain the rationale behind estimates, and clearly
distinguish them from 'solid' data."<sup id="r1"><a href="#n1">1</a></sup> The rule is not that
every date must be sure. The rule is that a reader must be able to see which dates are sure,
and why.

### 1.1 What the existing guide books do, and what the guide books cannot do

The field already has good guide books. John Dunning's *On the Air: The Encyclopedia of
Old-Time Radio* describes about 1,500 programs. *On the Air* gives broadcast histories,
times, networks, advertisers, and main workers.<sup id="r2"><a href="#n2">2</a></sup> The *Handbook
of Old-Time Radio*, by Jon Swartz and Robert Reinehr, serves collectors in a similar
way.<sup id="r3"><a href="#n3">3</a></sup> Together *On the Air* and the *Handbook of Old-Time
Radio* establish the record at the level of the *program*, and establish the record well. The
method described here depends on the work in both guide books. Step 2 of the procedure in
section 4 uses a guide book to learn a program's usual day and hour.

But the guide books do not date a single surviving recording, and the guide books never set
out to do so. The subject of the guide books is the program and the program's run. The
problem here is the single item — a sound file, in trading now, whose place inside a
program's run is not known. A guide book can tell a cataloger that *Greatest Story Ever Told*
was a Sunday program, and that *Greatest Story Ever Told* ran in 1948 and also in 1955. A
guide book cannot say which Sunday produced the recording a cataloger holds. In the case
studied in section 5.4, the program-level record fits the correct date and the wrong date
equally well.

So the hole is built into the problem. The hole is not a failure of the guide books. To date
a single item, a cataloger needs evidence about the single item. For the old-time radio
period, the only published record that works item by item, day by day, is the newspaper
radio log. The paper argues that the newspaper radio log should be treated as the primary
source for dating old radio recordings. The paper also argues that each record should show
which listings a cataloger read, and what the listings proved.

## 2. The collection

The Archive of American Radio is a public catalog of American broadcast recordings, and runs
not for profit. In September 2026 The Archive of American Radio published 259,915 separate
broadcast records across 6,114 programs.<sup id="r4"><a href="#n4">4</a></sup> Every broadcast record
has a fixed web address in the form `/series/{program}/episodes/{item}`. Where evidence has been found, a broadcast record also carries a formal citation, a set of newspaper listings, and a
set of trade magazine listings.

The collection did not arrive all at once. The collection gathers material from the main
old-time radio distribution channels, and so carries every dating problem described above, at
full strength. Much of the cataloging work puts aside a date passed along and never tested with
a date proved against published evidence from the time.

Two facts about the published catalog can be measured directly. Both facts matter for the
method.

**For the classic period, the evidence is nearly complete.** The Archive of American Radio
holds sixty records, chosen at random, whose item name carries a date between 1930 and 1959.
Fifty-nine of the sixty records — 98 percent — carry at least one newspaper page image or
trade magazine page image.<sup id="r5"><a href="#n5">5</a></sup> For the years when the American
radio log was a daily feature, the evidence the method needs is almost always present.

**Old dates survive beside corrected dates.** Of the 259,915 records, 44,566 carry a date in
the item name, in the form `YYYY-MM-DD`, passed along from the file name of a source
collection. In a random sample of forty date-carrying records from the whole collection, two
records — 5 percent — carry a name date that does not agree with the broadcast date the
record states. Inside the 1930 to 1959 window, a single record in sixty did not
agree.<sup id="r6"><a href="#n6">6</a></sup> Both samples are small, and both numbers should be read
only as a rough size. Together the two samples suggest that one to two thousand records still
carry an old date in the item name after the catalog has corrected the date. Section 5.4
examines a record in the same state.

**More than a quarter of the collection has no date at all.** In a random sample of 150
records from the whole catalog, 108 records — 72 percent — carry a broadcast date. Forty-two
records — 28 percent — carry no date.<sup id="r7"><a href="#n7">7</a></sup> Across 259,915 records,
forty-two in 150 points to about seventy-two thousand items held with no date.

The link between dating and evidence in the same sample is the more useful result. Of the 108
records with dates, 101 records — 93.5 percent — carry at least one page image. Of the
forty-two records without dates, **two** carry a page image. So records without dates are
not, as a rule, records where a cataloger looked for evidence and failed. Records without
dates are records where no cataloger has looked yet. In The Archive of American Radio, dating
a record and gathering evidence for the record are nearly the same job. The forty records in
the sample with no date and no evidence stand for work still to be done, and not for a limit
on what the method can reach.

### 2.1 The same broadcast, held more than once

The published catalog shows a connected problem. The Archive of American Radio holds three
separate records for Winston Churchill's speech on Anglo-American unity, given at Harvard on
6 September 1943. Two records carry the date Monday, 6 September 1943, and six page images
each. The third record carries no date and no evidence at
all.<sup id="r8"><a href="#n8">8</a></sup>

Three records for a single broadcast is normal for a collection built from trading. The same
broadcast arrives by several routes, and each copy carries a different amount of information.
Removing the extra copies is itself a dating problem. The record without a date is a weak
record whose answer The Archive of American Radio already holds, completely proved, in two
records cataloged separately. Sorting out a group of copies is among the most useful work a
cataloger can do, because the evidence has already been found.

## 3. Kinds of evidence

Three kinds of evidence can fix the date of a surviving broadcast recording. The three kinds are not the same in how often each exists, in how exact each is, and in the kind of mistake each
invites. A method that treats the three kinds as equal will produce wrong answers that look
sure.

### 3.1 Newspaper radio logs

The newspaper radio log is the main kind. From the early 1930s, large American daily
newspapers printed a day-by-day radio schedule, called the radio log. The radio log listed
the station, the time, and the program name for the coming day. Radio logs are the closest
thing the period produced to a complete published record of what went on the air.

Four newspapers carry the method: the *New York Times*, the *Washington Post*, the *Chicago
Daily Tribune*, and the *Los Angeles Times*. The value of the four newspapers comes less from
any single newspaper than from the fact that the four newspapers are separate. Each newspaper
built a radio log from information supplied separately by networks and stations, for a
different city and a different time zone. A program name and hour printed in all four
newspapers is confirmed in a way a single listing can never be.

Radio logs have three weaknesses, and each weakness must be answered openly. First, a radio
log records the *schedule* — what a network meant to broadcast — and not the *broadcast*.
When a news bulletin went out instead, the radio log and the broadcast no longer agree.
Second, the radio log went to the printer before the broadcast day began, so the radio log
shows an earlier deadline. Third, radio logs are printed in very small print in narrow newspaper columns. Computers read small print badly, so mistakes enter easily.

### 3.2 Trade magazines

*Broadcasting*, the industry weekly magazine, and magazines like *Broadcasting*, recorded
changes to schedules, advertiser assignments, network line-ups, and special events. Trade
magazines give a kind of detail the daily newspapers never tried to give. Trade coverage is
weekly, not daily, and so covers fewer dates. But trade coverage is much richer in the detail
that separates a broadcast from another broadcast inside a program's run. Trade coverage is
the natural second source when a radio log proves a program aired, but not which episode
aired.

### 3.3 Evidence inside the recording

A recording contains information that can be dated. A word about the news sets an earliest
possible date. An advertiser's words, and the campaign named in the words, can be placed
inside a season. The announcer's name, the network's wording, and the wording of station
identification all changed over time, and every change can be dated. Sometimes a broadcast
names a current event. A broadcast may give an election result, a war report, or the result
of a game. Then the recording can often be placed to the exact day from what the recording
says. No outside listing is needed.

Evidence inside a recording is the only kind that speaks to the *broadcast* rather than the
schedule. So evidence inside a recording decides the question when a radio log and a
recording do not agree. Evidence inside a recording is also the kind most easily mixed up by
a later repeat, a later copy, or an Armed Forces Radio Service reissue of the same program.

## 4. The method

The whole method is one sentence. Ignore the date on the file, work out roughly which week
the recording is from, look that week up in the newspaper radio listings, and count how many
of the four newspapers list it. The count is how sure you get to be.

The rest of this section is why each step is there.

**Ignore the date on the file.** The date on a file name was put there by someone you cannot
ask. Start from that date and the work quietly changes shape: instead of asking what night
the show went out, you start looking for a reason to keep the date you were handed. And you
will find one, because a plausible date usually has a plausible story around it. So write the
date down, mark where it came from, and set it aside. Do not use it again until the end.

**Work out roughly when the recording was made.** Listen to it. A war report, an election
result, a sports score, a joke about the news — any of these can fix the week. The advertising
helps too, because campaigns ran in seasons. So does the announcer, and so does the wording of
the station identification, which changed over the years. None of this gives a day. It gives a
window, and a window is enough to go looking.

**Look that week up in the newspaper radio listings.** Every big American daily printed the
next day's radio schedule: station, time, show. Four papers matter here — the *New York
Times*, the *Washington Post*, the *Chicago Daily Tribune*, the *Los Angeles Times*. Read the
listings for each candidate date, in each paper.

**Count the newspapers that list the show at the same hour.** The count is the answer, and it
becomes the mark on the record:

- **A** — two or more newspapers list the broadcast
- **B** — one newspaper lists the broadcast
- **C** — no newspaper lists the broadcast, but the recording and the show's known schedule
  point to the same date
- **D** — an estimate, given as a range, with the reasoning written down
- **E** — the date came with the file and nobody has checked it

Counting works because the four papers are separate. Each built its listings in its own city,
in its own time zone, from copy the networks sent to each paper on its own. Four papers
agreeing is four people who never spoke to each other saying the same thing. That is why two
is the line for Mark A.

**Mark E is the one that matters.** A catalog with no way to write down "nobody has checked
this" will turn a stranger's guess into a fact, and will do it silently. Section 5.4 is exactly
that: 13 February 1955 never had evidence behind it. It had repetition behind it.

Two things to watch. Two papers printing the same network schedule are one source, not two —
the network wrote it once. And a recording that fits a listing may still be a later repeat, so
check whether the advertising has been cut out, because that is what the Armed Forces Radio
Service did when it reissued a show.

## 5. Examples

### 5.1 A broadcast with a great deal of evidence: 1 September 1939

The BBC announcer Alvar Lidell reported the German invasion of Poland. The Archive of
American Radio holds the report as a recording of forty-five
seconds.<sup id="r9"><a href="#n9">9</a></sup> The catalog record for the Alvar Lidell report
carries ten separate page images. The ten page images include radio logs from the *Chicago
Daily Tribune*, the *New York Times*, and the *Los Angeles Times*. The ten page images also
include two different listings from the *Washington Post*. All the listings are for Friday 1
September 1939. The same issue of *Broadcasting* completes the
set.<sup id="r10"><a href="#n10">10</a></sup>

The Alvar Lidell date is a Mark A date by a wide margin. The Alvar Lidell record shows the
best the method can do. A broadcast that mattered around the world, on a day that mattered
around the world, is listed everywhere, and confirming the date is easy.

### 5.2 The same day, a different recording

The Archive of American Radio catalogs Ignacy Jan Paderewski's speech about the coming German
invasion separately, on the same day. The Paderewski record rests on the same group of
evidence: a *Chicago Daily Tribune* radio log for Friday 1 September 1939, and the same issue of *Broadcasting*.<sup id="r11"><a href="#n11">11</a></sup> The Alvar Lidell record and the
Paderewski record are useful together for what the two records show about method. A single group of
listings for a single day can support several separate broadcast records. The single piece of evidence is the printed page, and not the recording.

### 5.3 A broadcast with little evidence: 1932

Now compare a recording from 1932. The record for Franz von Papen's speech as chancellor, on
13 August 1932, carries three page images. The three page images come from the *New York
Times* and the *Washington Post* only.<sup id="r12"><a href="#n12">12</a></sup> The *Chicago Daily
Tribune* listing and the *Los Angeles Times* listing that support the 1939 records are
missing. The von Papen date is supported, but the confirmation behind the von Papen date is
much less. A system that reported the Alvar Lidell record and the von Papen record the
same way would give a false picture of the von Papen record.

### 5.4 A case that corrects a passed-along date by seven years

The value of the method lies in correction, and not in repetition. A clear case is the
*Greatest Story Ever Told* episode "They Did Not Receive Him."

The recording is traded under a file name holding the date 13 February 1955. The 1955 date
also survives in the item name inside The Archive of American Radio, passed along from the
source collection. But the catalog record gives the broadcast date as **Sunday, 29 February
1948** — an error of almost seven years in the traded
date.<sup id="r13"><a href="#n13">13</a></sup>

Six separate listings from the time support the 1948 date. The six listings are radio logs in
the *New York Times*, the *Washington Post*, the *Los Angeles Times*, and the *Chicago Daily
Tribune*. A separate "Radio Highlights" item in the *Chicago Daily Tribune* is the fifth
listing. The matching issue of *Broadcasting* is the sixth listing. All six listings are for
Sunday, 29 February 1948.<sup id="r14"><a href="#n14">14</a></sup> The date for "They Did Not
Receive Him" is a Mark A date.

Two features make the *Greatest Story Ever Told* case worth studying.

First, **the day of the week cannot settle the case.** *Greatest Story Ever Told* was a Sunday
program, and both possible dates fall on a Sunday. Both 13 February 1955 and 29 February 1948
were Sundays. A method resting on a program's known weekday would have accepted the passed-along
date without trouble. Only the six supporting listings tell the two possible dates from each other. The
*Greatest Story Ever Told* case is the argument for requiring published evidence instead of
reasoning from a schedule.

Second, **the correct date is a leap day.** 29 February 1948 exists only because 1948 was a
leap year. Leap days are a known source of passed-along mistakes. A cataloger putting a date back
together from a damaged or partial file name will usually change a strange-looking 02-29 into
a date that looks usual. The error in the *Greatest Story Ever Told* record runs in exactly
the direction a leap day predicts.

The citation inside The Archive of American Radio already points to a web address without the
wrong date. So the corrected date is what the catalog states and cites. The 1955 text survives
only in the item name — an example of the file name problem described in section 1, now
repeated inside a catalog that has otherwise corrected the date.

## 6. The evidence is not equally thick across the period

The three examples above suggest a shape, and the shape can be measured. The Archive of
American Radio holds fourteen cataloged broadcasts from a single news program, covering 1932
to 1939. Across the fourteen broadcasts, the number of separate page images attached to a
record rises strongly with the year of the broadcast. The correlation is 0.899 across fourteen
records.<sup id="r15"><a href="#n15">15</a></sup> The mean number of page images by year:

| Broadcast year | Average page images | Records |
|---:|---:|---:|
| 1932 | 3.0 | 3 |
| 1933 | 4.0 | 3 |
| 1935 | 4.0 | 1 |
| 1936 | 6.0 | 1 |
| 1937 | 5.0 | 1 |
| 1939 | 7.8 | 5 |

Two reasons are possible, and the measurements cannot separate the two reasons. The first
reason is that newspaper radio logs really did grow across the 1930s, as radio became a mass
medium and the radio log grew from a new thing into a fixed daily feature. The second reason
is that collecting of newspaper images by The Archive of American Radio is simply more
complete for the later 1930s. Under the second reason, the shape describes the archive and not
the period.

The difference between the two reasons matters for how the marks should be read. Under the
first reason, a Mark B date from 1932 is as strong as the evidence of 1932 permits. Under the
second reason, a Mark B date from 1932 is only a record The Archive of American Radio has not
finished working on. Telling the two reasons apart would require a newspaper index
independent of the archive's own holdings. No one has read an independent index.

The finding is limited in another way as well. The finding rests on fourteen records, all from
a single program of international news. International news is exactly the kind of program most
likely to appear in every city's radio log. A general statement about the whole collection
would need a sample spread across program kinds and networks.

## 7. Limits

**A schedule is not a broadcast.** Every date the method takes from a newspaper proves what
was scheduled. Programs were pulled, above all during news events, and news events are exactly
when the collection is richest and the reasoning weakest.

**Repeats and reissues.** A recording that agrees with a listing may be an Armed Forces Radio
Service reissue, a regional repeat, or a later copy of the same program. Only evidence inside
the recording can tell a reissue from a first broadcast, and evidence inside the recording is
not always present.

**Computer reading errors.** Radio logs are printed in dense, small print. Computers read
small print badly. A mistake in the evidence passes into the catalog with no sign.

**Small samples.** The finding in section 6 rests on fourteen records from a single program.
The collection-wide numbers in section 2 come from the published catalog, and completing the
numbers needs the cataloging history held by The Archive of American Radio.

**Copying the evidence.** For the old-time radio period, the newspaper pages that provide the
evidence are still under copyright. The paper cites listings by newspaper, date, and kind of
listing. The paper does not copy the newspaper
pages.<sup id="r16"><a href="#n16">16</a></sup>

## 8. Publishing the reasoning, not only the answer

A mark for the strength of evidence solves nothing while the mark lives only in a cataloger's
private notes. The failure described in section 1 is exactly that careful decisions flatten
into bare claims as recordings pass from collector to collector. For a mark to survive the
trading networks that will pass old radio recordings on, the mark must be published with the
record and attached to the record firmly.

Three rules follow. The Archive of American Radio follows all three rules.

**The citation belongs to the record, and not to a report.** Each broadcast record carries a
formatted citation and a list of supporting listings beside the date, at the same web address.
A reader who reaches a broadcast record reaches the evidence for the record. Publishing the citation beside the date is what makes the undated copy in section 2.1 easy to see. Two records state a date with six listings each. A third record states nothing. Any reader can see the difference.

**The item name must not carry a competing claim.** Section 5.4 is a warning. The catalog
holds the corrected date, cites six listings for the corrected date, and points the citation
at a web address without the wrong date. Yet the item name still reads `1955-02-13`, and the
item name is what another computer reading web addresses will collect. An item name containing
a date makes a claim, whether or not a cataloger meant a claim. The estimate in section 2
suggests one to two thousand records are in the same state. Dates belong in fields, and not in
names.

**Missing information must be recordable.** Twenty-eight percent of the collection has no
date, and the catalog says so rather than supplying a likely year. Saying so is the same rule the Association for Recorded Sound Collections asks of discographers in the passage
quoted in section 1. For a date with no support, the other choice is not an empty space to
fill in. The other choice is a clear statement that the question is still open. Mark E holds
the statement. The number of two in forty-two — records without dates almost never carrying
page images — is what makes Mark E useful, because the number identifies undated records as
work waiting to be done.

## 9. Conclusion

The dating problem in old-time radio is not mainly a problem of missing evidence. The evidence
largely exists. The evidence sits in the radio logs of four large daily newspapers and in the
trade press, and most of the evidence is now on computers. The problem is that the information
kept with old radio recordings has no way to show the difference between a date proved against
published evidence and a date passed along without evidence. So a proved date and a passed
along date can no longer be told from each other.

The answer offered here is simple. Attach a mark for the strength of evidence to every date.
Keep a visible category for dates passed along and never tested. Cite the exact published
listing behind every confirmed date. Applied across 259,915 records, the marking system turns
a trading collection into a collection scholars can cite.

## Sources for the discography

This section states the sources the method rests on, what each one covers, and where the
coverage fails, so that a reader can follow the same steps described in section 4.

**Newspaper radio logs.** Four large daily newspapers carry this method: the *New York Times*
(New York), the *Washington Post* (Washington), the *Chicago Daily Tribune* (Chicago), and the
*Los Angeles Times* (Los Angeles). Each built its log from information supplied for its own
city and time zone. That is why agreement between them counts as confirmation rather than
repetition. Where a newspaper printed both, two kinds of listing are counted separately: the
daily *radio log*, which is a complete schedule, and *radio highlights*, which is a selected
column written by an editor. The *Chicago Daily Tribune* record for 29 February 1948,
discussed in section 5.4, provides one of each, and they count as two listings because they
were produced separately.

Coverage is not even. Within the archive's holdings, the number of listings available for a
broadcast rises across the 1930s, from an average of 3.0 in 1932 to 7.8 in 1939 in the sample
studied (section 6). For the years 1930 to 1959, 98 percent of the cataloged broadcasts
sampled carry at least one page image. Across the whole collection the figure is 68.7
percent. Almost all of that difference comes from records with no date, of which only two in
forty-two carry any page image at all.

**Where the newspaper images came from.** The catalog presents each newspaper listing as a
page image, labeled with the newspaper, the city, the date, and the kind of listing. It does
not record where the scan came from. The published version of this paper should name that
source. It should say whether each run was read in a library newspaper database, on
microfilm, or in a scan collection made by someone else. It should also say what is known
about how complete that source is. Where the source is a private collection rather than a
library, the paper should say so plainly. It should give what is known about the
collection's history and about any rights it claims. A reader cannot follow steps whose
starting point is unnamed, and recording it is the single most useful addition this apparatus
still needs.

**Trade magazines.** *Broadcasting*, the industry weekly, is read through WorldRadioHistory,
which the catalog names and cites as the source of the scan. Trade coverage is weekly rather
than daily. It is used for schedule changes, advertiser assignments, network line-ups, and
special events — that is, for separating one broadcast from another inside a run when the
daily logs give only a program name. In the sample studied, *Broadcasting* appears for the
better documented broadcasts and is missing for the thinner ones. It adds to the daily logs.
It does not replace them.

**Evidence inside the recordings.** The recordings are the archive's own holdings. Each has a
permanent web address in the form `/series/{program}/episodes/{item}`, and each carries a
formal citation. Where this paper cites evidence inside a recording, it means sound that can
be heard in the archive's copy.

**Where coverage fails.** Three failures are known, and any reader following this work should
expect them. Radio logs record the schedule and not the broadcast, so a replaced program
breaks the match — and that happens most often during news events, which is when this
collection is richest. Logs went to press before the broadcast day began. And they are
printed in dense, small type that computers read badly, so mistakes in the evidence pass
silently into the catalog. Adding more newspapers does not fix any of these, because all four
newspapers share them.
