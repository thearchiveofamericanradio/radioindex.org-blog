### Key Historical & Archival Insights Extracted

1. **Babe Ruth's Outfield Masterclass on Radio (August 12, 1947)**: Less than a year before his death, George Herman "Babe" Ruth joined Dodgers announcer Red Barber on his *Baseball Clinic* over WHN/Mutual. Speaking in his gravelly, authoritative voice, Ruth provided an invaluable instructional primer on playing right field: breaking down how to read wind off stadium flags, why an outfielder must watch the crack of the bat rather than the batter's swing, and how to throw ahead of the runner. It survives as one of the finest athletic masterclasses preserved in golden age sports broadcasting.
2. **The 1945 Premature Japanese Surrender Wire Flash (August 12, 1945)**: On Sunday evening, August 12, 1945, at 9:34 PM EWT, radio networks across North America cut abruptly into ongoing musical programs to broadcast seven electric words received over the United Press teletype: *"Japan accepts surrender terms of the Allies."* Sirens wailed and impromptu street celebrations ignited nationwide before an emergency UP flash two minutes later ordered editors to kill the transmission, citing an unauthorized wire breach. This preserved recording captures the raw tension of network anchors frantically balancing breaking unverified copy against wartime censorship protocols.
3. **Richard Tucker's Summer Breakthrough (August 12, 1945)**: Having made his Metropolitan Opera debut only seven months earlier in January 1945, Brooklyn-born tenor Richard Tucker took over the nationwide airwaves as guest star on NBC's *John Charles Thomas Show*. Tucker's electrifying delivery of Gioachino Rossini's Neapolitan tarantella *"La Danza"*, conducted by Victor Young with Ken Darby's choral backing, signaled Tucker's rapid ascent to becoming America's preeminent dramatic tenor of the post-war era.
4. **Hollywood A-Listers in 'The Devil and Miss Jones' (August 12, 1946)**: Van Johnson and Donna Reed—both riding massive box-office stardom following World War II—teamed with veteran character actor Guy Kibbee for Lady Esther's *Screen Guild Theater*. The adaptation expertly transposed Frank Ross and Norman Krasna's screwball labor comedy to radio, raising funds for the Motion Picture Country House while delivering sparkling social satire.
