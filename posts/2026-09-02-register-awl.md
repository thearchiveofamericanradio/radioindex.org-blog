## Abstract

A considerable body of American radio broadcasting survives as sound recordings, and this
material circulates among private collectors as digital audio files. A significant
proportion of it carries no broadcast date, and in many instances the only documentation
accompanying a recording is its file name. File names are inherently unstable: they are
rewritten at each transfer, shortened by the systems that store them, and, where the text
can no longer be read, replaced by a cataloger's estimate. The date is therefore lost, and
a significant share of the dates that do survive are demonstrably erroneous.

This paper specifies a method for establishing the true broadcast date of such a recording.
The primary evidence is the newspaper radio log, the day-by-day schedule of radio programs
printed in American daily newspapers, and the method derives its authority from the
comparison of logs published independently in several cities. Trade press listings and
evidence internal to the recording itself supplement that primary source.

The paper reports the results obtained through the application of this method at The
Archive of American Radio, a published catalog of 259,915 broadcast records distributed across 6,114 programs.
Twenty-eight percent of those records remain undated.

The paper further proposes a framework for making the strength of the evidence behind each
date explicit, so that a date confirmed by several independent contemporary listings is no
longer recorded in the same form as an estimate, or as a date transmitted from another
collection and never subsequently validated.

A single case demonstrates the method in operation. Six independent contemporary listings
displace one recording's accepted date by almost seven years, and the day of the week
cannot resolve the question, because both candidate dates fall on a Sunday and the program
was broadcast on Sundays.

The paper also reports one significant limitation. Within the sample analyzed, the number
of listings available for a broadcast increases sharply with the year across the 1930s, a
trend measured as a correlation of 0.899 across fourteen records. The evidence is
not uniformly distributed across the period, and any dating framework must indicate that
variation rather than obscure it behind a uniform claim of fact.

**Keywords:** old-time radio, discography, broadcast dating, newspaper radio logs, provenance,
audio archives, metadata

## 1. The problem: a body of evidence that lost its dates

American radio survives principally because private collectors retained it. The programs
were recorded on transcription discs and subsequently on tape, the copies were themselves
copied, the copies were traded, and the traded copies were eventually converted into
digital files. This sequence continued for approximately seventy years, and throughout that
interval no library or archive imposed a uniform convention on the documentation
accompanying each recording. The result is a large body of historically significant material that carries
almost no reliable documentation.

The most significant failure is the loss of the date. An undated recording is
significantly weaker as evidence, because it cannot be assessed against the news of its own
week, linked to an advertiser's campaign, or located within the run of the program that
generated it. Nor can it be used to demonstrate what a network in fact transmitted on a
given evening. Radio was live and scheduled, and then it was gone; the date is therefore
not one attribute among many, but the attribute that converts a sound file into a
historical document.

Dates are lost through several distinct mechanisms, and it is useful to separate them,
because each requires a different response.

**File names break down.** Within trading networks the file name carries the documentation.
Participants revise the name at each transfer to conform to their own conventions,
operating systems reduce names that exceed their limits, and characters that are illegal
on one platform are removed by the person transferring the file. Cataloging software alters
others. A date written as `xx-xx-xx` may survive one transfer; it does not survive three.

**Erroneous dates are transmitted forward.** Where a date is present it has more often been
transmitted than validated. A collector assigns a date that appears reasonable, and every
subsequent recipient of the file copies that date forward. The date acquires an appearance
of authority in direct proportion to the number of times it has been repeated, and no
mechanism within a trading network ever eliminates an erroneous date.

**Programs are dated; recordings are not.** Many holdings record only the years across
which the program ran, such as 1952 to 1961. That is a fact about the program, and it is
not a fact about the recording.

**Estimates are stored as facts.** When a cataloger estimates a date, the estimate is
written into the same field, in the same format, as a validated date. Nothing marks the
difference, and the difference is therefore lost at the first transfer.

This paper treats the last of these mechanisms as the principal problem, because a careful
method can correct it. The Association for Recorded Sound Collections states the principle
explicitly in its *Guidelines for Discographies*: "estimates are preferable to omitting the
information. Discographers should explain the rationale behind estimates, and clearly
distinguish them from 'solid' data."<sup id="r1"><a href="#n1">1</a></sup> The requirement is
not that every date be conclusive; the requirement is that the reader be able to identify
which dates are secure, and on what grounds.

### 1.1 What the existing reference works do, and what they cannot do

The field already possesses authoritative reference works. John Dunning's *On the Air: The
Encyclopedia of Old-Time Radio* documents approximately 1,500 programs, together with their
broadcast histories, their scheduled times, their networks, their sponsors, and their
principal contributors.<sup id="r2"><a href="#n2">2</a></sup> The *Handbook of Old-Time Radio*,
by Jon Swartz and Robert Reinehr, serves the collecting community in a comparable
capacity.<sup id="r3"><a href="#n3">3</a></sup> Together these publications establish the
record at the level of the *program*, and they do so authoritatively. The method specified
here depends on that work: step 2 of the procedure in section 4 consults precisely this
category of source in order to obtain a program's scheduled day and hour.

These works do not, however, date an individual surviving recording, and they were never
designed to do so. Their subject is the program and its run, whereas the problem addressed
here is the individual item — one sound file, currently in distribution, whose position
within that run is unknown. A reference work can indicate to a cataloger that *Greatest
Story Ever Told* was a Sunday program broadcast in 1948 and again in 1955, but it cannot
specify which Sunday generated the recording in hand. In the case analyzed in section 5.4,
the program-level record is equally consistent with the correct date and with the erroneous
one.

This limitation is therefore structural, and it does not constitute a failure of the existing
literature. Dating an individual item requires evidence about that individual item, and for
this period the only published record operating at that level, day by day, is the newspaper
radio log. This paper argues that the radio log should be treated as the primary source for
dating these recordings, and that each record should make explicit which listings were
consulted and what those listings establish.

## 2. The collection

The Archive of American Radio is a public, not-for-profit catalog of American broadcast
recordings. In September 2026 it published 259,915 discrete broadcast records distributed across 6,114
programs.<sup id="r4"><a href="#n4">4</a></sup> Every broadcast record occupies
a persistent address of the form `/series/{program}/episodes/{item}`, and, where the
evidence permits, each record also carries a formatted citation, a set of newspaper
listings, and a set of trade press listings.

The collection was not assembled at one time. It aggregates material from the principal
old-time radio distribution channels, and it consequently exhibits every dating failure
specified above, at full strength. A significant proportion of the cataloging effort
consists of replacing a date that was transmitted rather than validated with one
established against contemporary published evidence.

Two properties of the published catalog can be measured directly, and both are significant
for the method.

**For the classic period the available evidence is nearly complete.** The archive holds
sixty records, selected at random, whose item name carries a date between 1930 and 1959.
Fifty-nine of them — 98 percent — carry at least one newspaper or trade press page
image.<sup id="r5"><a href="#n5">5</a></sup> For the years in which the American radio log
was a daily feature, the evidence this method requires is almost invariably available.

**Earlier dates persist alongside corrected ones.** Of the 259,915 records, 44,566 carry a
date in the item name, in the form `YYYY-MM-DD`, transmitted from the file name of a source
collection. In a random sample of forty such records taken from the whole collection, two —
5 percent — carry a name date inconsistent with the broadcast date the record itself
specifies. Within the 1930 to 1959 interval, one record in sixty was
inconsistent.<sup id="r6"><a href="#n6">6</a></sup> Both samples are small, and the
proportions should be read only as approximate. Taken together they indicate that one to
two thousand records still carry an earlier date in the name after the catalog has corrected
the date itself, and section 5.4 analyzes one such record in detail.

**More than a quarter of the collection is undated.** In a random sample of 150 records
taken from the whole catalog, 108 — 72 percent — carry a broadcast date, and forty-two — 28
percent — carry none.<sup id="r7"><a href="#n7">7</a></sup> Across 259,915 records, that
proportion indicates approximately seventy-two thousand items held without a date.

The correspondence between dating and evidence within that sample is the more useful
result. Of the 108 dated records, 101 — 93.5 percent — carry at least one page image, whereas of
the 42 undated records, **two** do. Undated records are not, as a category, records in
which evidence was sought and not found; they are records that no one has yet examined. In
this collection, dating a record and assembling its evidence constitute very nearly the
same operation. The forty records in the sample carrying neither a date nor any evidence
therefore represent work still to be undertaken, and not a limit on what the method can
reach.

### 2.1 The same broadcast, held more than once

The published catalog exhibits an analogous problem. The archive holds three discrete
records for Winston Churchill's speech on Anglo-American unity, delivered at Harvard on 6 September
1943. Two of them carry the date Monday, 6 September 1943, together with six page images
each, whereas the third carries neither a date nor any supporting
evidence.<sup id="r8"><a href="#n8">8</a></sup>

This condition is normal in a collection aggregated from trading networks. The same
broadcast arrives by several routes, and each copy carries a different volume of supporting
documentation, so that resolving the copies against one another is itself a dating problem.
The undated record is an impoverished record whose answer the archive already holds, fully
validated, in records it cataloged separately. Resolving such groups is among the most
productive tasks available to a cataloger, precisely because the underlying evidence has
already been obtained.
